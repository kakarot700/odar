# Does Raising the Minimum Wage Reduce Employment? An Assessment of the Empirical Evidence and Points of Disagreement Among Economists

## Executive Summary and Position Statement

The minimum wage is one of the most intensively studied and most fiercely contested policies in labor economics. The question of whether raising it reduces employment does not have a single, unanimous answer, but the weight of the most carefully conducted syntheses of the U.S. evidence suggests a qualified, concrete answer: **raising the minimum wage does reduce employment, and the reduction falls most heavily on the least-experienced, least-skilled, and most directly affected workers, even when aggregate employment effects appear small**. This conclusion is consistent with competitive labor-market theory, is supported by the large majority of core estimates extracted from the published U.S. literature, and is reinforced by findings on hours, hiring selectivity, and technological substitution that a simple headcount measure of "employment" misses ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf)). At the same time, the evidence does not support the stronger claim that any minimum wage increase will cause large-scale job loss; in labor markets with monopsony-like features, or when increases are moderate relative to local wages, wage gains can be substantial with little measurable headcount reduction ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)). Economists disagree not only about magnitudes, but about which studies to trust, which populations to study, which margins of adjustment to measure, and — fundamentally — how to weigh employment losses against wage and poverty gains.

## Theoretical Frameworks and Their Predictions

### The Competitive Labor-Market Model

In standard competitive models, the minimum wage functions as a legal price floor on labor. When set above the market-clearing wage, it reduces the quantity of labor demanded and increases the quantity supplied, creating unemployment among low-skilled workers ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)). Early textbook estimates implied substantial elasticities: a 10 percent increase in the minimum wage was thought to reduce teenage employment by 1 to 3 percent ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)), a result also echoed in later policy reviews that described time-series studies as "typically" finding that a 10 percent minimum wage increase reduces teenage employment by one to three percent ([American Action Forum, 2026](https://www.americanactionforum.org/insight/the-2026-state-minimum-wage-increases-and-their-potential-costs-and-consequences/)).

### The Monopsony Alternative

Monopsony models — in which employers have wage-setting power — predict a different result. In such markets, workers are paid below their marginal revenue product, and a moderate minimum wage can raise both wages and employment simultaneously; the effect depends on how far above the monopsony wage the minimum is set ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)). Monopsony conditions are argued to be common in rural hospitals for nurses, fast-food chains in small towns, meatpacking plants in isolated areas, and historically company towns where a single firm dominated employment ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

### Why Theoretical Debates Persist

This theoretical disagreement is not new. Stigler (1946) acknowledged that a higher minimum wage could theoretically raise employment under monopsony but argued that the competitive character of low-wage industries made displacement of low-wage labor the more likely outcome; Lester (1947) countered that reasoning about labor markets as though they were commodity markets was itself a source of error ([Neumark & Wascher, n.d.](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). The crucial practical point is that real labor markets typically sit between the two benchmarks, owing to search frictions, geographic mobility costs, and varying degrees of employer concentration; this heterogeneity is one reason empirical results vary so widely across studies and settings ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

## The Empirical Evidence on Employment Effects

### Historical Estimates and Early Empirical Revisions

The modern empirical literature is dominated by research that exploits subnational variation in U.S. minimum wages beginning in the early 1990s, when states began legislating floors above the federal minimum — developments that added statistical variation to policy variables and enabled a reexamination of the old consensus ([Neumark & Wascher, n.d.](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). The most famous early study in this new wave, Card and Krueger (1994), compared New Jersey fast-food restaurants (where the minimum rose from $4.25 to $5.05) with Pennsylvania and found no significant employment decrease, a finding widely interpreted as challenging the traditional view ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)). This landmark result helped launch decades of debate, but its generalizability has been questioned on grounds of single-industry focus and the large relative size of the New Jersey increase ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

**Table 1. Selected empirical estimates on minimum wage and employment**

| Source | Setting | Finding | Key Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Card & Krueger (1994), as reported in O'Connell (2026) | NJ vs. PA fast-food; $4.25 → $5.05 | No significant employment decrease | Single industry; large relative increase; results may not generalize |
| Time-series studies (1982 study, via UC Irvine review reported in American Action Forum, 2026) | U.S. teen labor market | 10% MW increase → 1–3% reduction in teen employment | Time-series designs; confounded by other macro changes |
| Upjohn Institute synthesis of recent research (n.d.) | Multiple U.S. studies, 2010s–2020s | MW reduces job growth over several years; effects strongest for younger workers and low-wage-worker-intensive industries | Varies by research design and context |
| CBO (2021) projection, via O'Connell (2026) | Federal $15 by 2025 | Median: 1.4M jobs lost; 17M workers receive raises; 900K lifted out of poverty | Wide confidence intervals; depends on implementation timeline |
| Belman & Wolfson meta-analysis, via Upjohn Institute (n.d.) | >200 empirical studies | Offers "the strongest evidence yet" on employment, wages, poverty, inequality, and gender effects | Synthesis-level conclusions contested by other reviewers |
| Neumark & Shirley (2022) | Core estimates from full published U.S. subnational literature | Clear preponderance of negative estimates; stronger for teens, young adults, less-educated, directly affected workers | Excludes estimates authors do not view as credible; industry-level evidence less one-sided |
| EPI survey (2026) of 166 economists | $15+ federal/state proposals | Nearly three-quarters oppose $15 proposals, citing job losses, higher prices, automation | Advocacy-commissioned survey; measures opinion, not causal estimates |

### Meta-Analyses and Literature Reviews: Divergent Summaries

A striking feature of the minimum wage literature is not merely that studies disagree, but that expert summaries of the same literature disagree ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)). Summaries range from "it is now well-established that higher minimum wages do not reduce employment," to "the evidence is very mixed with effects centered on zero," to "most evidence points to adverse employment effects" ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)).

The Upjohn Institute points to the meta-analysis by Belman and Wolfson covering over 200 empirical studies as "the strongest evidence yet on the effects of the minimum wage on employment, wages, poverty and inequality, and effect by gender" ([Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage)). A separate review by Neumark and Wascher, encompassing more than 100 studies published since the 1990s, reaches a different emphasis: while acknowledging a wide range of estimates and a lack of consensus on magnitudes, the review concludes that the oft-stated assertion that recent research fails to support the traditional view is "clearly incorrect," because "a sizable majority of the studies surveyed... give a relatively consistent (although not always statistically significant) indication of negative employment effects," and "among the papers we view as providing the most credible evidence, almost all point to negative employment effects," both in the United States and in many other countries ([Neumark & Wascher, n.d.](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)).

### The "Myth or Measurement" Synthesis and Core-Estimate Findings

The most methodologically explicit recent synthesis is Neumark and Shirley (2022), who assembled the entire set of published studies using U.S. subnational minimum wage variation and extracted the core estimates that support each paper's conclusions, in most cases relying on responses from the original authors rather than on tabulated estimates that papers do not treat as credible ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf)). Their central conclusions are:

- There is a clear preponderance of negative estimates in the literature ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)).
- Evidence of negative employment effects is stronger for teens and young adults, and stronger still for the less-educated ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)).
- Evidence from studies of directly-affected workers points even more strongly to negative employment effects ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)).
- Evidence from studies of low-wage industries is less one-sided, which Neumark and Shirley interpret as consistent with labor–labor substitution within industries — meaning that relatively weak industry-level effects can mask larger gross job losses for specific worker groups ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf)).

**Table 2. Distribution of core employment elasticity estimates (Neumark & Shirley, 2022)**

| Statistic | Share of estimated employment elasticities |
|---|---|
| Negative point estimate | 78.9% |
| Negative and significant at the 10% level or better | 53.9% |
| Negative and significant at the 5% level or better | 46.1% |

These figures directly contradict summaries that the literature shows "no adverse effects" or is balanced around zero, and Neumark and Shirley note that many popular summaries rely on tabulated estimates that the authors of the original papers did not regard as credible, such as panel specifications without fixed effects ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf)). The Upjohn Institute, by contrast, highlights its own recent research showing that the pass-through of minimum wage increases to prices is smaller than previously thought, that small raises lead to slower wage growth for low-wage workers, that the minimum wage reduces job growth over a period of several years — with effects strongest for younger workers and industries with a higher proportion of low-wage workers — and that worker hours decrease while capital investment rises following minimum wage increases ([Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage)).

### Effects on the Most Vulnerable Groups

The convergence across independent research programs is notable on one point: younger and less-educated workers bear the largest employment costs. Upjohn's synthesis finds effects strongest for younger workers and low-wage-worker-intensive industries ([Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage)). Neumark and Shirley find the strongest negative evidence for teens, young adults, the less-educated, and directly affected workers ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)). Reviews cited by the American Action Forum report that studies focusing on the least-skilled groups, including teenagers, "provide overwhelming evidence of stronger disemployment effects," and cite a 2018 Mercatus Center study finding higher minimum wages to be the "predominant factor" in the decline of teenage employment, especially among 16–17-year-olds since 2000 ([American Action Forum, 2026](https://www.americanactionforum.org/insight/the-2026-state-minimum-wage-increases-and-their-potential-costs-and-consequences/)). The American Action Forum frames this as a training-loss problem as well as an income problem, since displaced young workers also forgo entry-level on-the-job training that supports career growth ([American Action Forum, 2026](https://www.americanactionforum.org/insight/the-2026-state-minimum-wage-increases-and-their-potential-costs-and-consequences/)).

### Historical Correlations and Advocacy-Side Evidence

Advocacy sources emphasize evidence consistent with weak aggregate disemployment. Business for a Fair Minimum Wage notes that the real minimum wage peaked in 1968 at $13.86 (2022 dollars) while the unemployment rate fell from 3.8 percent in 1967 to 3.6 percent in 1968 and 3.5 percent in 1969, and that unemployment fell to similar levels after the 1996–1997 increases; it quotes *Business Week* (2001) observing that "many economists have backed away from the argument that minimum wage [laws] lead to fewer jobs" ([Business for a Fair Minimum Wage, 2022](https://www.businessforafairminimumwage.org/resource/research-shows-minimum-wage-increases-do-not-cause-job-loss)). These are descriptive time-series correlations rather than causal estimates, however, and they coincide with broader macroeconomic conditions (including tight labor markets) that independently push unemployment down — a classic identification problem that careful empirical research seeks to address through comparison groups, fixed effects, and border-discontinuity designs.

## Methodological Considerations Behind Divergent Conclusions

The disagreement among economists is partly methodological. Researchers using different methods and control groups often come to different findings ([Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage)).

**Table 3. Methodological choices that drive divergent conclusions**

| Methodological Dimension | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Research design | Early time-series studies are confounded by macroeconomic trends; panel data with state and time fixed effects, synthetic control methods, and border discontinuities offer different identification strength and can yield different estimates from the same policy episodes |
| Choice of control groups | Results depend heavily on how researchers construct comparison states or regions; trends in low-wage labor markets differ across states for reasons unrelated to minimum wage laws |
| Estimate selection | Summaries that pool all reported estimates (including specifications the authors do not endorse) can look more neutral than syntheses restricted to authors' preferred or "core" estimates, as Neumark and Shirley argue ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf)) |
| Population studied | Aggregate all-worker or industry-level results can mask larger gross job losses for teens, less-educated workers, or directly affected workers, owing to within-industry labor–labor substitution ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf)) |
| Margin of adjustment measured | Headcount employment is only one margin; hours, benefits, prices, hiring standards, and automation may absorb adjustment that headcount studies miss ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)) |
| Labor-market structure | Competitive versus monopsony settings predict different signs for moderate increases; real markets fall on a continuum determined by employer concentration, search costs, and geographic mobility ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)) |
| Size and context of increase | Moderate increases (up to roughly 59 percent of the local median wage) show small or zero employment effects in U.S. evidence, while larger increases carry greater job-loss risk; geographic variation in the binding relevance of a federal minimum (e.g., $7.25 binds more in low-cost states like Mississippi or West Virginia than in California or Massachusetts) compounds this ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)) |

## Employment Is Not the Only Margin of Adjustment

Even studies finding small employment effects may understate the total adjustment burden on workers and firms, because employment headcounts are not the only margin; firms may reduce hours, cut non-wage benefits such as employer-provided health insurance or meals, raise product prices, increase hiring selectivity, or accelerate automation of routine tasks ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)). The Upjohn Institute's research on establishment responses finds precisely this pattern: a decrease in worker hours and an increase in capital investment following minimum wage increases ([Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage)). Upjohn also finds that price pass-through is smaller than previously assumed, while small raises can lead to slower wage growth for low-wage workers and reduce job growth over multi-year horizons ([Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage)). On poverty and distribution, Upjohn research finds less clear results for the minimum wage as an antipoverty tool in disadvantaged neighborhoods, consistent with the observation that the minimum wage is an imprecise antipoverty instrument — not all minimum-wage workers belong to poor families, and not all poor families contain workers who would benefit from a higher floor ([Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage); [O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)). Projections illustrate the trade-offs concretely: a 2021 CBO projection of raising the federal minimum wage to $15 by 2025 yielded a median estimate of 1.4 million jobs lost alongside 17 million workers receiving raises and 900,000 people lifted out of poverty, with wide confidence intervals and results sensitive to the implementation timeline ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

## Where Economists Disagree: Empirical Evidence and Normative Weights

The disagreement is partly empirical and partly normative. Empirically, labor markets vary — some resemble competitive models, others have monopsony features — and genuinely different identification strategies produce different results, because it is difficult to isolate the minimum wage's effect from other simultaneous economic changes ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)). Normatively, economists weigh trade-offs differently: some place greater weight on employment risk, others on wage gains and distributional outcomes ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)). As one synthesis of the debate states plainly, "the minimum wage debate is as much about values as about evidence," because reasonable economists disagree about how to balance employment effects, poverty reduction, labor market efficiency, and the distribution of gains and losses ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

**Table 4. Survey evidence on economists' views of large minimum wage increases**

| Survey | Sample and Question | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Chicago Booth IGM Expert Panel (2015), as reported in Neumark & Shirley (2022) | Question on the employment rate for low-wage workers under a gradual federal $15 minimum wage by 2020 | Weighted responses: strongly agree (9%), agree (25%), uncertain (37%), disagree (29%), strongly disagree (0%) |
| Employment Policies Institute (2026) | 166 U.S. economists surveyed March–April 2026 on $15+ federal and state proposals | Nearly three-quarters oppose a $15 federal minimum wage, citing job losses, higher prices, and more automation |
| Deaton and Diamond (as quoted in Neumark & Shirley, 2022) | Published statement on recent minimum wage research | "The weight of evidence showing that previous, modest increases in the minimum wage had little or no negative effects on the employment of low-wage workers" |

A related empirical and normative contest involves policy design. Many economists regard the Earned Income Tax Credit as a more targeted antipoverty tool because it directly subsidizes low-income families and work rather than raising employer wage costs, and because it does not create the same modeled employment trade-offs; others note that the EITC requires government funding, may partially subsidize low-wage employers, and does not help workers outside the tax system, and many economists view the two policies as complements rather than substitutes ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

## Synthesis and Conclusion

Several conclusions can be drawn with confidence from the evidence assembled here:

1. **On average, and especially for vulnerable groups, raising the minimum wage reduces employment.** The careful core-estimate synthesis by Neumark and Shirley finds 78.9 percent negative point estimates, with negative and statistically significant estimates for more than half of studies at conventional levels, and the strongest effects for teens, young adults, the less-educated, and directly affected workers ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf)). Independent reviews report similar directional findings, with a "sizable majority" of studies indicating negative effects and "almost all" of the most credible studies pointing negative ([Neumark & Wascher, n.d.](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). Upjohn's own recent research on job growth over several years, establishment responses, hours cuts, and capital investment is directionally consistent ([Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage)).

2. **Magnitude is highly context-dependent, and moderate increases can produce small aggregate effects.** U.S. evidence suggests that moderate increases — up to roughly 59 percent of the local median wage — have small or zero aggregate employment effects, while larger increases carry greater risk, and industry-level estimates are less one-sided than estimates for directly affected or vulnerable groups because of within-industry substitution ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/); [Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)). Landmark studies such as Card and Krueger (1994) that find no significant employment decline in specific settings are important but not general-purpose evidence ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

3. **The claim that the literature "establishes no job loss" is not well supported by careful extraction of preferred estimates.** Summaries that pool all reported estimates, including specifications original authors do not consider credible, can misrepresent the direction of the core evidence ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf)). Conversely, the claim that any increase necessarily causes large job losses is also overdrawn, because market structure, increase size, and non-headcount margins matter enormously ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

4. **Employment headcounts understate total adjustment.** Hours reductions, benefit cuts, price increases, elevated hiring selectivity, and accelerated automation of routine tasks are all documented or theorized responses that mean even "small" measured employment effects may mask substantial burdens on low-wage workers and firms ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/); [Upjohn Institute, n.d.](https://www.upjohn.org/research-effects-raising-minimum-wage)).

5. **The debate is irreducibly partly normative.** Policy surveys show genuine division — the Chicago Booth IGM panel was closely split and heavily uncertain in 2015, while a 2026 EPI-commissioned survey found nearly three-quarters of 166 economists opposing $15 proposals ([Neumark & Shirley, 2022](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28388/revisions/w28388.rev2.pdf); [Employment Policies Institute, 2026](https://epionline.org/studies/data-snapshot-three-quarters-of-economists-oppose-15-minimum-wage-proposals/)). Reasonable economists disagree because they weigh employment effects, wage gains, and poverty outcomes differently; evidence can narrow the range of plausible outcomes but cannot resolve a value question about how society should balance competing goals ([O'Connell, 2026](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/minimum-wage-economics/)).

**My assessment**, grounded in the sources reviewed here, is that the most defensible reading of the evidence is that the minimum wage is a real but imperfect wage-increasing instrument whose costs fall disproportionately on the workers it is most often intended to help: young, less-educated, and directly affected low-wage workers. Moderate increases can raise wages substantially without large measured headcount losses in aggregate industry data, but the core estimates in the U.S. literature — and the non-headcount margins of adjustment — point consistently to disemployment pressure concentrated among the most vulnerable. Policy proposals for large jumps (such as a federal $15 or higher floor, or the numerous 2026 state increases) should therefore be evaluated case by case, with explicit attention to local labor-market structure, the size of the increase relative to median wages, and complementary tools such as the EITC, rather than relying on universalized claims that either job loss or job loss-free wage gains are settled facts.
