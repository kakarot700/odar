# Does Raising the Minimum Wage Reduce Employment? A Synthesis of the Empirical Evidence

## Introduction

Few questions in labor economics have generated as much controversy as the employment effect of the minimum wage. Since the early 1990s, a large body of research—now described as the "new minimum wage research"—has revisited this question using new data, natural-experiment designs, and state-level policy variation ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). The question remains policy-relevant today: the Democratic National Committee's recent platform included raising the federal minimum wage to $15 per hour, prompting renewed debate about whether such hikes cost jobs ([MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/)).

This report synthesizes what the empirical evidence says, drawing on the most comprehensive narrative review in the field ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)), a more recent NBER working paper testing the monopsony explanation ([NBER WP 26101, 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26101)), a 2024 Springer chapter reviewing effects from employment to well-being ([A Literature Review on the Effects of the Minimum Wage, 2024](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-67604-8_4)), and a 2024 policy commentary summarizing survey and federal-budget evidence ([MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/)).

**My assessment:** The best-supported conclusion is that raising the minimum wage generally *does* reduce employment for some low-skill workers, and this is the modal finding in credible studies; however, the *magnitude* of the effect varies dramatically by context—most importantly by how binding the minimum wage is, which workers are studied, and the concentration of the local labor market. In many settings the average employment effect is small or statistically indistinguishable from zero, a pattern that recent evidence attributes in part to monopsony power. But large increases—particularly proposals that raise the wage floor far above prevailing low-wage levels, or that eliminate the tip credit—carry meaningful employment losses concentrated among the least-skilled, youngest, and most entry-level workers.

## Background: Theory and the Origins of the "New Minimum Wage Research"

### Theoretical ambiguity

Economic theory does not deliver an unambiguous prediction. Even in the neoclassical competitive model, the effect of the minimum wage on employment "will depend on, among other things, the elasticities of substitution across different types of workers and cross-elasticities of demand across different types of goods" ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). Yet, as the review notes, empirical tendencies tend to align better with one model or another, and some empirical findings are more consistent with competitive models than with alternatives such as monopsony ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)).

This ambiguity has deep historical roots. Stigler (1946) acknowledged that a higher minimum wage could theoretically raise employment under monopsony but argued that the competitive character of low-wage industries made displacement of low-wage labor the more likely outcome. Lester (1947) countered that reasoning about labor markets "as though they were commodity markets" leads to erroneous conclusions ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). Early empirical work, including Goldfarb (1974), suggested that wage-floor increases were having adverse effects on the employment opportunities of low-skilled workers ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)).

### What changed in the 1990s

Three developments triggered the new research: renewed public debate about raising the nominal federal minimum; a growing number of state legislatures raising state minimum wages above the federal level, which added statistical variation in the policy variables used in minimum wage research; and a 1991 conference at Cornell's ILR Institute and Princeton's Industrial Relations Section, organized by Ronald Ehrenberg and Alan Krueger, whose papers appeared in an October 1992 symposium in the *Industrial and Labor Relations Review* ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)).

## What the Comprehensive Literature Review Finds

### The headline conclusions

Neumark and Wascher's review ([2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)) covers more than 100 studies published since the 1990s in the United States and other countries. Their key conclusions are as follows:

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Consensus status | There is "a wide range of existing estimates and, accordingly, a lack of consensus" about overall effects on low-wage employment ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). |
| Direction of the modal result | "A sizable majority of the studies surveyed in this monograph give a relatively consistent (although not always statistically significant) indication of negative employment effects of minimum wages" ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). |
| Highest-quality studies | Among studies the authors regard as providing the most credible evidence, "almost all point to negative employment effects, both for the United States as well as for many other countries" ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). |
| Positive effects | The authors find "very few – if any – studies that provide convincing evidence of positive employment effects of minimum wages," especially for broader worker groups rather than narrow industries ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). |
| Least-skilled workers | Studies focusing on the least-skilled groups "provide relatively overwhelming evidence of stronger disemployment effects for these groups" ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). |

The authors explicitly reject the oft-repeated claim that recent research "fails to support the traditional view that the minimum wage reduces the employment of low-wage workers," calling that assertion "clearly incorrect" ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)).

### The first-wave results that launched the debate

The initial studies generated sharply divergent estimates. Neumark and Wascher's state-and-time-series analysis produced disemployment effects similar to the earlier consensus; Card's (1992a) California study found no employment effect; and Card (1992b) and Katz and Krueger's (1992) Texas fast-food survey found positive effects ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). In the well-known New Jersey–Pennsylvania fast-food study, Card and Krueger (1994) found a positive and statistically significant employment effect, with an estimated elasticity of 0.73 in one specification, which they interpreted as "inconsistent with the predictions of a conventional competitive model of the fast-food industry" ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)).

Neumark and Wascher object to the characterization of single-state designs as natural experiments with valid control groups, arguing instead that cross-state comparisons are contaminated by broader economic trends ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). They also caution that "evidence on employment effects from a single sector is not necessarily informative about the employment effects of minimum wages more generally" ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)).

### Methodological caution about narrative reviews

The authors deliberately eschew a formal meta-analysis, arguing that studies estimate many different types of employment effects using widely varying approaches and that "meta-analysis is even less useful when the underlying theory does not provide uniform predictions about the effects of the minimum wage in every study" ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). They acknowledge that a narrative review "introduces an element of subjectivity," and organize the evidence in four tables highlighting studies they regard as most convincing ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). This candor is important: their ranking of "most credible" studies is partly a judgment call, which is one substantive reason qualified researchers reach different conclusions.

## Why Average Effects Are Often Near Zero: Labor Market Concentration and Monopsony

A major explanation for why many recent studies find near-zero average employment effects is that low-wage employers often have market power. Recent theory predicts that when wages are set below marginal productivity—under monopsony—employers can raise wages without layoffs; but "systematic evidence directly supporting this explanation was lacking" until recently ([NBER WP 26101, 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26101)).

Using occupation-level labor market concentration data with "fine spatial variation" covering the entirety of the United States, and focusing on a key low-wage retail sector, that study finds:

- "More concentrated labor markets – where wages are more likely to be below marginal productivity – experience significantly more positive employment effects from the minimum wage."
- "Increases in the minimum wage are found to significantly decrease employment of workers in low concentration markets."
- "Minimum wage-induced employment changes become less negative as labor concentration increases, and are even estimated to be positive in the most highly concentrated markets" ([NBER WP 26101, 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26101)).

The authors conclude that their findings "provide direct empirical evidence supporting the monopsony model as an explanation for the near-zero minimum wage employment effect documented in prior work," and that aggregate estimates "may mask heterogeneity across different levels of labor market concentration" ([NBER WP 26101, 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26101)). This is a crucial qualification: a near-zero average effect can coexist with significant job losses in more competitive local markets.

## Effects in Specific Policy Scenarios: Tipped Workers and Large Proposed Increases

Policy design matters as much as the wage level. A 2024 commentary reports several scenario-specific results:

- **Tipped workers:** Dr. Neumark's research finds that "every $1 tipped wage hike causes a 6% decline in tipped restaurant employment and a similar decline in earnings for tipped restaurant workers," which the commentary describes as an even worse outcome when tip-credit elimination is included in a wage hike ([MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/)).
- **$15 federal proposal:** The Congressional Budget Office's analysis of raising the minimum wage to $15 nationwide with tip-credit elimination "could cost nearly a million jobs and send over a hundred thousand workers into poverty" ([MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/)).
- **Expert survey:** A survey of 160 U.S. labor economists conducted by the Economic Policy Institute found that "83% believe the federal minimum wage should be below $15 per hour – many citing negative impacts of wage hikes on teen and entry-level employment, small business survival, and inflationary pressures" ([MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/)).

It is worth noting that the commentary itself comes from an advocacy-oriented site, so its framing of the "consensus" should be read with care; however, the underlying items it cites—the CBO projection and the EPI economist survey—are independent analyses, and the direction of both aligns with the review evidence on least-skilled and entry-level workers ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf); [MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/)).

## Beyond Employment: The Emerging Evidence on Well-Being

A 2024 Springer chapter reviews the literature on minimum wage effects "from employment to well-being," indicating that research attention has expanded well beyond job counts ([A Literature Review on the Effects of the Minimum Wage, 2024](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-67604-8_4)). That chapter catalogues recent work on who benefits from minimum wage increases (Lopresti & Mumford, 2016), effects on employer-sponsored insurance provision (Meiselbach & Abraham, 2023), health and related behaviors (Neumark, 2024), and wage discrimination (Neumark, 2004), and summarizes a major survey of employment effects (Neumark, 2018) as well as an exploration of conflicting evidence on minimum wages in developing countries (Neumark & Corella, 2021) ([A Literature Review on the Effects of the Minimum Wage, 2024](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-67604-8_4)). Notably, the existence of a dedicated review on "conflicting evidence" in developing-country contexts reinforces that the empirical picture remains contested in some settings and that results do not travel uniformly across countries.

## Where Economists Disagree

| Dimension of disagreement | View aligned with competitive prediction | View aligned with monopsony / new research | Nature of the dispute |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnitude of disemployment | Substantial losses for low-skill workers (Neumark & Wascher-type estimates) | Near-zero average effects in many settings | Choice of samples, time periods, and control groups; cross-state spillovers |
| Interpretation of first-wave findings | Card–Krueger studies are sector-specific and not generalizable | Card–Krueger studies are evidence against the competitive model | Scope: single sector vs. economy-wide effects ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)) |
| Role of labor market structure | Competitive low-wage labor markets dominate | Concentrated markets explain why average effects are small | New concentration data reveal large heterogeneity ([NBER WP 26101, 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26101)) |
| Synthesis method | Narrative weighting of "most credible" studies | Formal meta-analysis or null-effect framing | Neumark & Wascher decline meta-analysis because theory does not give uniform predictions ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)) |
| Policy relevance | Large hikes (e.g., $15) pose real job-loss risk | Moderate hikes can raise earnings with limited job loss | Scenario design matters—tipping sector and tip-credit elimination are especially affected ([MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/)) |

Qualified researchers reach different conclusions for substantive, not merely ideological, reasons: they differ in which theoretical model they believe describes low-wage labor markets, in how much weight they place on single-sector vs. broad-sample evidence, and in how they handle methodological challenges such as invalid control groups, pre-existing state trends, and spillover effects across state borders ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)).

## My Assessment

Weighing the evidence above, my concrete position is:

1. **Direction of the effect:** In the modal credible study, a minimum wage increase reduces employment for some segment of the low-skill labor force. Neumark and Wascher's finding that a "sizable majority" of studies point to negative effects—and that among the most credible studies "almost all point to negative employment effects"—is difficult to dismiss ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). I therefore treat "raising the minimum wage has no employment cost" as an unsupported claim, particularly for larger increases.

2. **Magnitude:** The average effect is often small and statistically insignificant. This is genuinely important. Monopsony theory and the concentration evidence explain why: in markets where employers have wage-setting power, a wage floor can raise pay with little or no job loss, and employment effects can even turn positive in highly concentrated markets ([NBER WP 26101, 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26101)). Aggregate averages, however, can mask meaningful job losses in more competitive local markets, which is where low-skill workers are most exposed ([NBER WP 26101, 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26101)).

3. **Who bears the cost:** Disemployment effects concentrate among the least-skilled, younger, and entry-level workers ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). Scenario-specific analyses are consistent: a $1 tipped wage hike is associated with a 6% decline in tipped-restaurant employment, and a CBO analysis of a $15 minimum wage with tip-credit elimination projects the loss of nearly a million jobs and over 100,000 additional people in poverty ([MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/)).

4. **The bottom line:** Moderate increases that remain close to prevailing wage distributions appear, in many settings, to generate limited net job loss—though even then the least-skilled workers face some risk. Substantially larger increases, especially those combined with tip-credit elimination, carry serious, well-documented employment and earnings risks. The prudent reading of the literature is that the minimum wage is a trade-off, not a free lunch: it can raise earnings for many while reducing employment opportunities for the most vulnerable.

---

## Directions for Future Research

Several gaps warrant further work. First, finer spatial and occupation-level data on labor market concentration—as used in the NBER working paper—should be applied to additional sectors and countries, because they reveal heterogeneity that aggregate estimates obscure ([NBER WP 26101, 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26101)). Second, the field needs better tools for synthesizing heterogeneous estimates than either a bare meta-analysis or a subjective narrative review, given that theory itself does not yield uniform predictions ([Neumark & Wascher, 2007](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12663/w12663.pdf)). Third, the expanding well-being literature—covering health, employer-sponsored insurance, and poverty transitions—should be integrated with employment effects so that policy evaluation reflects full welfare consequences ([A Literature Review on the Effects of the Minimum Wage, 2024](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-67604-8_4)). Finally, tipping-sector dynamics and cross-country comparability remain areas where evidence is conflicting and additional research is needed ([MinimumWage.com, 2024](https://minimumwage.com/2024/08/what-do-economists-really-say-about-wage-hikes/); [A Literature Review on the Effects of the Minimum Wage, 2024](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-67604-8_4)).
