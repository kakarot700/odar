# Research Report: Does raising the minimum wage reduce employment? Summarize what the empirical evidence says, including where economists disagree.

## Answer (written only from the certified findings below)
Economists do not agree on whether raising the minimum wage reduces employment, and they acknowledge genuine uncertainty about a proposed $15 federal minimum [[1]](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/what-economists-think-about-a-15-minimum-wage). The disagreement is well documented across studies of minimum wage employment effects in the United States, with research since the early 1990s relying heavily on subnational minimum wage variation within the country [[3]](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388). Even what the literature as a whole concludes is poorly established: there is an absence of agreement on how economists summarize the body of evidence on employment effects [[3]](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388). The current federal minimum wage stands at $7.25 and has not been raised in a decade [[2]](https://www.npr.org/2019/07/08/739607964/-15-minimum-wage-would-boost-17-million-workers-cut-1-3-million-jobs-cbo-says).

Steve Kaplan of Chicago Booth strongly agreed that raising the wage would adversely affect the unemployment rate, arguing that a $15 rise makes entry-level and low-wage jobs very expensive [[1]](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/what-economists-think-about-a-15-minimum-wage). Quantitatively, a Congressional Budget Office study projected that raising the federal minimum wage to $15 by 2025 would put 1.3 million Americans out of work [[2]](https://www.npr.org/2019/07/08/739607964/-15-minimum-wage-would-boost-17-million-workers-cut-1-3-million-jobs-cbo-says).

Seattle raised its minimum wage to as much as $11 in 2015 and as much as $13 in 2016 [[4]](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20180578)[[5]](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23532); an evaluation of those phase-ins attributes more modest effects to the first wage increase [[5]](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23532). The facts available here do not fully answer the broader question, however, since they provide no detailed findings on how the Seattle phase-ins affected employment and hours, no summary of the wider research literature beyond noting its disagreement, and no basis for weighing job losses against wage gains in a single conclusion [[3]](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28388)[[5]](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23532).
