## Q5 — Does intermittent fasting lead to more weight loss than daily calorie restriction?
Site: duck.ai (Claude Haiku 4.5, Web Search ON — "Remove Web Search" chip present; response complete)
Model line shown: Claude Haiku 4.5
Timing: not instrumented (no wall-clock timestamps captured; rough seconds unknown — see note)

Full answer (verbatim, citation markers rendered as inline [label] links in the chat):

The research shows no significant difference in weight loss between intermittent fasting and daily calorie restriction in most cases — with a notable recent exception.

When total calories are matched between the two approaches, intermittent fasting produces nearly identical weight loss to continuous daily calorie restriction. A major 2026 Cochrane review examined 22 randomized controlled trials with nearly 2,000 participants and found the weight loss difference between the two methods was "statistically indistinguishable from zero." [Rutgers University] A 2025 meta-analysis of 20 studies reached the same conclusion: intermittent fasting is not superior to calorie restriction for weight loss when calories are equal. [ScienceDirect]

The Key Distinction: Calorie Equivalence Matters

The critical factor is whether the calorie deficit is truly matched. When intermittent fasting and calorie restriction involve the same weekly calorie reduction, they produce equivalent results. However, one recent study found an interesting exception.

The 4:3 Intermittent Fasting Exception

A 2025 study published in Annals of Internal Medicine found that a 4:3 intermittent fasting approach (eating freely 4 days per week, restricting calories heavily on 3 days) produced greater weight loss than daily calorie restriction: 7.6% average weight loss versus 5% in the continuous restriction group. [cuanschutz.edu]

The researchers designed both groups to have identical weekly calorie deficits (34%), yet the 4:3 pattern still outperformed. The researchers believe this might be a "sweet spot" between extreme fasting protocols (like alternate-day fasting, which can be too rigid) and milder ones (like 5:2, which may not create enough deficit). [cuanschutz.edu]

Practical Considerations

Adherence and Preferences
Beyond weight loss itself, what matters most is which approach you can sustain long-term. The Cochrane review noted that current evidence provides little information about whether one method is easier to maintain than the other. Some people find intermittent fasting psychologically easier because they don't count calories daily, while others experience higher hunger levels during fasting windows. [ScienceDirect] (this in-text chip was grouped — "Show citations" popup contained two URLs, listed in citation order below)

Other Health Markers
When weight loss does occur with either method, both approaches show similar improvements in blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar control. The 4:3 intermittent fasting study did show slightly more favorable changes in these cardiometabolic markers, though researchers noted these were secondary outcomes requiring larger studies for confirmation. [cuanschutz.edu]

Bottom Line
Whichever method results in a consistent calorie deficit is the "best" one for you. If daily restriction feels restrictive or unsustainable, intermittent fasting—particularly the 4:3 pattern—may be worth exploring as an evidence-based alternative. If you prefer steady, daily calorie management, that's equally effective. The real determinant of success is consistency and adherence over time.
