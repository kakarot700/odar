mRNA vaccines like Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna deliver synthetic messenger RNA that encodes a harmless piece of the target virus (e.g., the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2). Once injected into muscle cells, the mRNA is translated into the spike protein by the cell's ribosomes [1]. The protein is then displayed on the cell surface, which triggers the immune system to produce neutralizing antibodies and memory T/B cells. The mRNA itself is rapidly degraded shortly after translation, so it does not enter the nucleus or alter DNA [1]. Later, if the person encounters the actual virus, their immune system recognizes the spike protein and mounts a rapid defense.

Regarding immunity duration, for COVID-19, data show that while neutralizing antibody levels decline over several months, protective memory cells (B and T cells) remain. Short-term protection against infection wanes noticeably by 4–6 months after the primary series, but protection against severe disease and hospitalization remains substantial for at least 6 months, and is greatly extended by booster doses [2]. Longer-term studies indicate that immune memory may persist for at least a year following infection or vaccination, though new variants can evade some immune responses, necessitating periodic boosters [3]. For other mRNA vaccines in development, similar principles apply, as the durability of immunity will likely depend on the antigen and the speed of viral mutation.
