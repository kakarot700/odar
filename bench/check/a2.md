The 2008 global financial crisis was triggered by a confluence of factors, primarily the collapse of the U.S. housing bubble and its contagion through complex financial instruments. Starting in the early 2000s, low interest rates and lax lending standards fueled a surge in subprime mortgages—loans extended to borrowers with poor credit histories [1]. These loans were bundled into mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs), which were rated highly by credit agencies despite their underlying risk [2]. Financial institutions, including banks and shadow banks, relied heavily on short-term funding and excessive leverage, creating a fragile system vulnerable to housing price declines [1].

As interest rates rose and home prices fell starting in 2006–2007, subprime borrowers began defaulting in large numbers. The value of MBS and CDOs plummeted, causing severe losses at global financial institutions and triggering a panic in short-term credit markets [2]. The failure of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 intensified the crisis, leading to a global credit freeze and severe economic recession worldwide [3].
